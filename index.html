Research

Irrigation Makes It Rain? Climate Spillovers and Adaptation in India

Job Market Paper (draft available soon)

Can economic development change the climate and reshape its own trajectory? I study how large-scale irrigation infrastructure reshapes weather patterns and agricultural development in India. Combining five decades of administrative, climatic, and agricultural data, I exploit wind direction as a source of exogenous exposure to distant irrigation schemes. A 10 p.p. increase in irrigation, about half of India's Green Revolution expansion, raises dry-season rainfall by 5 mm per month and lowers temperatures by 0.3°C. Effects reverse during the monsoon, with declining rainfall and seven additional days of crop-damaging heat. Crop production rises by 5% in both seasons without yield gains, as farmers expand private irrigation and cropping intensity downwind. In the long run, these spillovers sustain smallholders while enabling larger farms to expand, polarizing the agricultural sector.

Publications

Might India's Use of Groundwater be Weakly Sustainable?

with D. Blakeslee, R. Fishman, and E. Zaveri · Journal of Environmental Economics and Management, 2026

In many parts of the world, groundwater is being extracted at rates that exceed natural renewal rates, leading many experts to conclude that groundwater mining is an unsustainable strategy for rural development. However, groundwater extraction could still be considered to be ‘weakly sustainable’ if it generates investments in man-made capital that offset the loss of natural ‘groundwater capital’ and enable income to be sustained even as the resource is depleted. In this paper, we examine whether the withdrawals of groundwater for irrigation in India have resulted in household-level investments in physical and human capital. Our empirical strategy compares villages in the same administrative units that overlie aquifers with different water storage capacities and, therefore, endowed with different levels of access to the resource. We find that greater access to the resource results in more irrigation, as expected, as well as higher asset wealth and educational attainment. The results suggest that deeming India’s irrigation-based rural development as unsustainable purely because of the decline in water tables may require reconsideration, provided that human capital is used to productively shift income from farm to off-farm sources.

Working Papers

Irrigation and the Redistribution of Human Capital

with D. Blakeslee and R. Fishman · Available upon request.

Can place-based policies shape the spatial distribution of human capital through selective migration? We use geographic discontinuities in irrigation infrastructure in India granular survey data to show that women living in irrigated areas have 2.3 additional months of schooling relative to their non-irrigated neighbors. With nearly 80% of women in our sample being migrants, we show that this difference is largely driven by the selective in-migration of better-educated women into irrigated areas for marriage purposes. By attracting more educated brides, irrigation infrastructure indirectly leads to a spatial sorting of human capital, potentially enhancing socioeconomic disparities directly resulting from infrastructure placement.

Work in Progress

Deforestation, Downwind Rainfall and Conflict

with T. Vendryes and J. Wolfersberger

Rainfall shocks shape development and conflict outcomes, yet their drivers are often assumed to be exogenous. In this paper, we show that deforestation in West Africa reduces precipitation in downwind areas and causes conflict. Combining satellite forest loss, wind trajectories, precipitation, and conflict events since the 2000s, we construct location-specific exposure to upstream deforestation using prevailing winds and exclude nearby pixels to isolate long-distance spillovers. We find that an increase in upstream deforestation indeed lowers precipitation, with effects extending to 200 km. Turning to development outcomes, we show that exposure to deforestation increases conflict intensity in downwind areas, driven primarily by civil unrest events, with effects persisting for up to six years.

Previous Research

Master 2 Thesis: Electricity Access and Agricultural Productivity in Madagascar under the supervision of Mathilde Maurel

This paper estimates the heterogeneous and dynamic effects of first-time electricity access on cropland productivity in Madagascar using satellite data in a staggered difference-in-differences setup. I show that while initial impacts may be negative, long-term benefits arise, particularly in villages located above productive aquifers.

Master 1 Thesis: Water Pollution and Infant Health in India under the supervision of Thomas Vendryes

This paper examines how water pollution affects child health in India, showing that higher levels of specific pollutants are associated with lower birth weight and poorer growth outcomes, based on a newly constructed water pollution level dataset matched with DHS data.
